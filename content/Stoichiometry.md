**Stoichiometry** is a concept in [[Chemistry]] that is usually taught in [[General Chemistry]]. Stoichiometry deals with the relationships of products and reactions in a chemical reaction.

# Balancing Chemical Equations

To balance chemical equations, the number of atoms of each element on both the reactant and product sides must be equal. This can be done by adding coefficients (preferably whole numbers) to the immediate left of the compound. Do not change the subscript as changing that will change the compound.

$$
\text{Reactant } \longrightarrow \text{ Product} \\ \\
\ce{AB + C -> AC + B}
$$

Notes:
[[Atoms#Polyatomic Ions|polyatomic ions]] can be treated as one element.
Example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{C2H6 + O2 &-> CO2 + H2O} \\
\ce{2C2H6 + 7O2 &-> 4CO2 + 6H2O}
\end{aligned}
$$

| Reactants |  Number of atoms   | Products |         Number of atoms          |
| :-------: | :----------------: | :------: | :------------------------------: |
|     C     | ${}2\times 2=4{}$  |    C     |        ${}1\times 4=4{}$         |
|     H     | ${}6\times 2=12{}$ |    H     |        ${}2\times 6=12{}$        |
|     O     | ${}2\times 7=14{}$ |    O     | ${}(2\times 4)+(1\times 6)=14{}$ |

# Dimensional Analysis

_See also: [[Measuring#Dimensional Analysis|Dimensional Analysis]]_
Dimensional Analysis is used in stoichiometry as working in moles is easier.

## Conversions

### Grams to Moles

$$
\text{Mass (g)}\times \frac{1mol}{\text{Molar mass (g)}} 
$$

### Moles to Grams

$$
mol \times \frac{\text{Molar mass (g)}}{1mol}
$$

### Particles to Moles

$$
\text{Particles}\times \frac{1mol}{6.022\times 10^{23}\text{ particles}} 
$$

### Moles to Particles

$$
mol \times \frac{6.022\times 10^{23}\text{ particles}}{1mol}
$$

Notes:

- Particles will either be: atoms, molecules or formula units depending on what is asked and given.
- Consider computing in one go instead of by parts as rounding off in between steps usually compound until the final answer, which may make your final answer deviate from the proper one.

# Limiting and Excess Reactant

To find the limiting or excess reactant, simply compute the amount of products each given amount of reactant will make and compare them. The reactant that made less products is the limiting reactant and will be the basis of the theoretical yield. The reactant that made more products is the excess. You can calculate how much excess reactant was leftover after the reaction by computing the amount consumed then checking the difference with the initial.

$$
\text{Initial amount}-\text{Leftover amount}=\text{Consumed amount} 
$$

# Yield

The **theoretical yield** can be computed by using the limiting reactant.
The **actual yield** is the amount made after the actual experiment.
The **percent yield** is computed as follows:

$$
\% \text{yield}=\frac{\text{Actual Yield}}{\text{Theoretical Yield}}\times 100\%
$$

Notes:
Percent yield less than 100% is common.
Percent yield over 100% can be attributed to error or wet/improperly dried product
